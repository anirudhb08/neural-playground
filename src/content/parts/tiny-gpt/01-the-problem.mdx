Start typing a message on your phone and a few suggested words appear above the
keyboard, offering to finish the sentence for you. Type into a search box and it
completes the query before you have. Ask ChatGPT anything and it writes back.

Those three feel like different sizes of thing. They are the same thing, and it
is smaller than it looks:

<div class="claim mt-7">
Given the text so far, what comes next?
</div>

That is the entire task. Not "understand this sentence", not "answer this
question" — just *what is the next symbol likely to be*, asked over and over,
each answer becoming part of the text for the next question.

It sounds trivial. It is worth ten minutes finding out why it isn't.

## Why not just look it up?

The obvious approach: keep every stretch of text you have ever seen, and when
you need to predict, find the same stretch and see what followed.

Below is that idea, measured. The slider controls how much of the sentence the
model is allowed to see. For each amount, it reports how many distinct
characters followed that exact run of text anywhere in a small body of text —
a **corpus**, which is just the word for the text a model learns from — and,
more importantly, how many times that run occurred at all.

<div class="mt-7"><GptWidget name="how-much-context" client:only="react" /></div>

Drag it and watch both numbers at once. The corpus is 1,552 characters — four
paragraphs — and the whole story is over in six:

| context | occurs | distinct characters seen after it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 character | 295 times | 21 |
| 2 characters | 32 times | 12 |
| 3 characters | 5 times | 4 |
| 4 characters | 3 times | 2 |
| 6 characters | **once** | — |

The narrowing is real: twenty-one plausible next characters become four. That
is the argument for looking further back, and it works.

But read the middle column while you do. The same characters that narrowed the
answer from twenty-one to four also cut the evidence from 295 examples to five.
By six characters there is one match left, and that one match is the sentence we
were trying to predict — it is the answer key, not evidence. For a sentence the
corpus has never seen, the count at six characters is zero.

<div class="claim mt-7">
Longer context makes prediction easier and evidence rarer, and the second effect
wins long before the first pays off.
</div>

These are not two stages. Every character that helps also costs, in the same
step, and the cost compounds faster than the help.

Nor is this an artefact of a small corpus. Scale it up and the wall moves a few
characters further out — it does not go away. There are more possible
twenty-character passages than have ever been written down, by an enormous
margin, so almost every context a model meets in real use is one it has never
seen exactly.

## Why not write rules instead?

The other obvious approach. Encode what you know about English — `q` is followed
by `u`, sentences end with a full stop, adjectives come before nouns — and
predict from that.

This fails in a way worth naming precisely, because it is the same way it failed
for handwriting in the [other tutorial here](/neural-networks/01-the-problem/).
You can read the sentence *the cat sat on the* and know that `mat` is more
likely than `mathematics`, but you cannot write down the rule you used. It
involves knowing what cats do, what sentences of that shape usually mean, and
what sounds right. Rules capture the parts of language that are mechanical, and
those are the parts that were never the difficulty.

## So what is left?

Neither remembering nor rules. What remains is to build something that has seen
a great deal of text and has extracted from it a *general* sense of what follows
what — one that can produce a sensible answer for a passage it has never
encountered, because it is not looking anything up.

That is what the rest of this tutorial builds, one piece at a time. Each piece
exists because the version without it fails in a specific way, and you will
watch each failure before the fix arrives.

## Is this really what the big models do?

Yes, and it is worth being clear about it because the answer surprises people.
A model that writes essays is running the same loop as your phone's keyboard:
predict the next symbol, append it, predict again. Nothing else is happening.

What separates them is how far back they can look, and how well they judge which
of the things they can see actually matters.

| | how far back it sees | parameters |
|---|---|---|
| The bigram in part 02 | 1 character | none at all |
| The model this tutorial builds | tens of characters | tens of thousands |
| GPT-2, 2019 | 1,024 tokens | 124 million |
| GPT-3, 2020 | 2,048 tokens | 175 billion |
| Current frontier models | hundreds of thousands of tokens | not published |

<aside class="aside measure mt-5">
The units in that column are not the same, which flatters the larger models
slightly. This tutorial works one character at a time, because a character is
the simplest symbol there is. Bigger models group characters into **tokens** —
roughly a common word or a chunk of one, three or four characters on average —
so a thousand tokens is a few thousand characters. The comparison is rough, and
rough is enough: the gap is factors of a thousand, not of four.
</aside>

The arc of this tutorial is that first column. You will start with one character
of memory, and every part after adds a way of using more of what came before.

## What can you actually make with a small one?

Not an essay-writer. Being straight about that matters, because the gap between
this and GPT-4 is real and no amount of enthusiasm closes it.

But a character-level model trained on a few megabytes of text — the size you
can train on a laptop in minutes — genuinely does the following, and people have
built all of them:

- **Text in the style of a corpus.** Train on Shakespeare and it produces
  plausible-looking pentameter with invented words. The classic demonstration,
  and still a good one.
- **Names.** Baby names, band names, place names, fantasy species. Anything
  where the goal is *plausible and new* rather than correct, and a short model
  with no memory of the wider world is exactly right for it.
- **Music**, written as text. ABC notation is a sequence of symbols with strong
  local structure, which is precisely what these models capture well.
- **Molecules.** SMILES strings are text, and small sequence models are used to
  propose candidate structures.
- **Code in a narrow syntax** — configuration files, regular expressions, a
  domain-specific language with few keywords.

The pattern is that anything expressible as a sequence of symbols with local
regularities is fair game. The model does not know it is producing music or
chemistry; it is predicting the next character, which is the only thing it ever
does.

<div class="claim mt-8">
The task never changes. Only how much you can see, and how well you weigh it.
</div>
