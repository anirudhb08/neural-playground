Start typing a message on your phone and a few suggested words appear above the
keyboard. Type into a search box and it completes the query before you have.
Ask ChatGPT anything and it writes back.

Those three feel like different sizes of thing. They are the same thing, and it
is smaller than it looks:

<div class="claim mt-7">
Given the text so far, what comes next?
</div>

That is the entire task. Not "understand this sentence", not "answer this
question" — just *what is the next symbol likely to be*, asked over and over,
each answer becoming part of the text for the next question.

It sounds trivial. The difficulty is that the answer can depend on anything:
the letter just typed, the word before it, or something written a paragraph
ago — and nothing announces in advance which one matters this time.

## Is this really what the big models do?

Yes, and it is worth being clear about it because the answer surprises people.
A model that writes essays is running the same loop as your phone's keyboard:
predict the next symbol, append it, predict again. Nothing else is happening.

What separates them is how far back they can look, and how well they judge which
of the things they can see actually matters.

| | how far back it sees | parameters |
|---|---|---|
| The bigram in part 02 | 1 character | none at all |
| The model this tutorial builds | tens of characters | tens of thousands |
| GPT-2, 2019 | 1,024 tokens | 124 million |
| GPT-3, 2020 | 2,048 tokens | 175 billion |
| Current frontier models | hundreds of thousands of tokens | not published |

<aside class="aside measure mt-5">
The units in that column are not the same, which flatters the larger models
slightly. This tutorial works one character at a time, because a character is
the simplest symbol there is. Bigger models group characters into **tokens** —
roughly a common word or a chunk of one, three or four characters on average —
so a thousand tokens is a few thousand characters. The comparison is rough, and
rough is enough: the gap is factors of a thousand, not of four.
</aside>

The arc of this tutorial is that first column. You will start with one character
of memory, and every part after adds a way of using more of what came before.

## What can you actually make with a small one?

Not an essay-writer. Being straight about that matters, because the gap between
this and GPT-4 is real and no amount of enthusiasm closes it.

But a character-level model trained on a few megabytes of text — the size you
can train on a laptop in minutes — genuinely does the following, and people have
built all of them:

- **Text in the style of a corpus** — a corpus being just the body of text a
  model learns from. Train on Shakespeare and it produces plausible-looking
  pentameter with invented words. The classic demonstration, and still a good
  one.
- **Names.** Baby names, band names, place names, fantasy species. Anything
  where the goal is *plausible and new* rather than correct, and a short model
  with no memory of the wider world is exactly right for it.
- **Music**, written as text. ABC notation is a sequence of symbols with strong
  local structure, which is precisely what these models capture well.
- **Molecules.** SMILES strings are text, and small sequence models are used to
  propose candidate structures.
- **Code in a narrow syntax** — configuration files, regular expressions, a
  domain-specific language with few keywords.

The pattern is that anything expressible as a sequence of symbols with local
regularities is fair game. The model does not know it is producing music or
chemistry; it is predicting the next character, which is the only thing it ever
does.

<div class="claim mt-8">
The task never changes. Only how much you can see, and how well you weigh it.
</div>
