The bigram in part 02 looked at exactly one character and forgot everything
else. Part 03 made the text into numbers so something better could read it.
Better means looking at more — which raises the question this part answers
first: **how much more?**

## Why not simply everything before it?

Because a model is a fixed piece of arithmetic. The same multiplications run on
every input, so every input has to be the same size, every time. "Everything so
far" is one character at the start of a book and three hundred thousand by the
end, and there is no shape that is both.

So you pick a number in advance and it never changes. The model sees that many
characters and not one more. It is called the **context window**, and in code it
is usually `block_size`.

<div class="claim mt-7">
Everything before the window may as well not exist. The model cannot see it,
and has no way of knowing it is there.
</div>

That is the same limitation the bigram had, moved. A bigram's window is one
character wide. Nothing in this tutorial ever removes the wall — the rest of it
is about pushing the wall further back and using what is inside it better.

## Where do the training examples come from?

Here is where it gets strange, and it is the reason models like this can be
trained on more text than anyone could ever label.

Take one chunk of text, one character longer than the window. Call the first
part `x` and the same run shifted one along `y`. Then `y[0]` is what followed
`x[0]`, `y[1]` is what followed `x[0]` and `x[1]` together, and so on — so one
chunk is not one training example. It is as many as the window is wide.

Widen the window and the count goes up with it, out of the same sentence. Move
the chunk and it is a different eight examples, out of the same sentence again.

## Nobody wrote the answers down

Look at what the model is being taught with. There is a question, `the ca`, and
an answer, `t`. That looks exactly like labelled training data — the kind
somebody sits down and produces.

Nobody produced it. The answer to *what follows `the ca`* is the character
sitting immediately after `the ca` in a sentence that was written for its own
reasons. The label was already there, and shifting by one is the whole of how it
was extracted.

<div class="claim mt-8">
The text is its own answer key.
</div>

Learning this way — where the answers are recovered from the data itself rather
than supplied alongside it — is called **self-supervised** learning. The name
makes it sound like a technique. It is really just this: shift by one.

This is worth comparing against the [other tutorial](/neural-networks/02-your-alphabet/),
where you drew each character and then told the network which one it was. That
labelling is the expensive part of most machine learning, and it is the reason
training data usually runs out long before compute does. Here there is none of
it. Any text at all is training data the moment you have it — which is why these
models could be trained on quantities of text that nobody could have annotated
in a hundred lifetimes.

## The off-by-one worth being careful about

`y[3]` is the answer after seeing **four** characters, not three. It is the
character that follows `x[0]` through `x[3]` inclusive.

That is easy to nod along to and easy to get wrong in code, and getting it wrong
does not crash anything. Forget the shift and `y[i]` becomes `x[i]` — the answer
is the character the model was just shown, so it learns to copy its own input,
which it can do perfectly. Every measurement you have says training is going
beautifully. The model is useless. Data-pipeline mistakes are quiet in exactly
this way.

The last cell below does the boundary arithmetic for the same reason. With 49
characters and a window of 8, the largest legal starting point is 40 — because
`y` for that row has to reach index 48, which is the last one there is.

## Why several chunks at once?

One more idea and then the code. Training on one chunk at a time works and
wastes the machine: the arithmetic for one row of eight characters does not
come close to occupying a processor that is built to do thousands of such
things simultaneously.

So chunks are stacked. Pick several random starting points, cut a chunk at each,
and put them in a rectangle — `x` and `y` both come out as *rows × window*. This
is a **batch**, and the only thing the rows have in common is that they are
being processed together.

<aside class="aside measure mt-5">
The rows do not interact. Nothing from row 0 reaches row 3, and the model would
give identical answers if you ran them one at a time. Batching is a throughput
trick and nothing else — which is worth holding on to, because when attention
arrives in part 07 and characters genuinely do start affecting each other, it
will matter that they only ever do so *within* a row.
</aside>
