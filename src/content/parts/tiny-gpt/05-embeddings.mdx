There is a dataset now: questions, answers, and a way to make more whenever they
are wanted. What there is not is a model that can improve. Counting produced a
tally, and a tally has nothing in it to adjust.

So the next job is a model that can be corrected. It runs into trouble on the
very first step, because of something
[noticed and walked past](/tiny-gpt/03-into-numbers/#the-numbers-are-labels-not-quantities)
when characters were turned into numbers.

## The numbers were never quantities

The vocabulary gave `t` the number 9 and `a` the number 1. Those numbers are
name tags. They were handed out in alphabetical order and they mean nothing else.

A network cannot treat them as name tags, because multiplying and adding is all
it does. Give it 9 and 1 and it will work out that 9 is nine times 1 — correctly,
as arithmetic. It will notice that 9 and 8 sit next to each other and conclude
that `t` and `s` are nearly the same thing. It will notice that 3 is midway
between 2 and 4 and conclude that `d` is somehow midway between `c` and `e`.

None of that is true about language. All of it is true about alphabetical order,
which is the only reason the numbers came out that way.

<div class="claim mt-7">
The numbers say which character. They say nothing about it. Arithmetic cannot
tell the difference.
</div>

## Give each character a row instead

Stop handing over one number for `t`. Hand over thirteen.

One number for each character that might come next, saying how strongly `t`
expects it. Do that for every character in the vocabulary and the result is a
grid — thirteen rows, thirteen columns, one row per character. Thirteen because
the sentence in the lab has thirteen different characters in it.

You have seen that grid before. It is exactly the shape of
[the table you built by counting](/tiny-gpt/02-counting-pairs/#what-does-count-the-pairs-mean).
Same size, same job. The difference is where the numbers came from.

<div class="mt-7"><GptWidget name="embedding-table" client:only="react" /></div>

Counting gave a table that is right about one sentence and can never become
anything else. This one starts as noise — press *roll it again* and it is
different noise, exactly as useless. But every number in it can be moved a
little at a time until it is right, and the counted one could not.

<aside class="aside measure mt-5">
The grid is called an **embedding table**, and looking a character up in it is
called an *embedding*. The word sounds like it should mean something grander
than fetching a row from a table. It does not. Later on the rows get wider than
the vocabulary and start carrying more than next-character scores, and that is
when the word starts to earn itself.
</aside>

## Looking up a whole batch at once

The batching function produces four rows of eight characters at a time — four
because that is the batch size, eight because that is the window.

Each of those thirty-two characters is a number, and each number gets swapped
for its row from the grid. One line of Python does the lot, with no loop.

Before, four rows of eight numbers. After, still four rows of eight — except
each of the eight is now thirteen numbers rather than one. Written as shapes,
`(4, 8)` becomes `(4, 8, 13)`.

That swap is the whole of what an embedding layer does in any library.

## Scores, not percentages

Those thirteen numbers per character are called **logits**. The quickest way to
see what sits where is to read the shape one index at a time:

| ask for | and you get |
|---|---|
| `logits[0]` | the first of the four rows — eight positions, thirteen numbers each |
| `logits[0][3]` | one position: the thirteen scores given after reading four characters of that row |
| `logits[0][3][6]` | one number: how strongly `h` is expected there, `h` being number 6 in the vocabulary |

So the last number in the shape always counts across the alphabet. Each group of
thirteen is one complete opinion, about one place, in one row.

Those opinions are unpolished. A logit can be any number at all, negative
included, and thirteen of them add up to nothing in particular. A score of 4.2
does not mean 420%. It only means *more than 1.7*.

They are left that way deliberately, for two reasons. Percentages get very small
very quickly and lose accuracy when they do, while raw scores do not. And the
next step is built to take raw scores anyway.

## Turning them into percentages, and measuring the damage

Both of these you have met in the other tutorial, and neither changes here:

- **[Softmax](/neural-networks/05-how-wrong/)** turns one group of scores into
  percentages. Make every score positive, then divide each by the total of the
  group. The order is preserved and the results add up to 100%.
- **[Cross-entropy](/neural-networks/05-how-wrong/)** says how bad that was, by
  looking only at the percentage given to the character that actually came next
  and ignoring the other twelve. The single number it produces is called the
  **loss**, and shrinking it is the whole of training.

What is different here is how many groups there are. One drawing used to produce
one group of scores. Now there are four rows of eight positions, so there are
thirty-two groups of thirteen, and each group is worked out entirely on its own.

That matters more than it sounds. If two positions ever shared a total, a
character's chances in one place would depend on what was happening somewhere
else in the sentence — which is not what any of this is supposed to mean. Each
position's percentages come from that position's own thirteen numbers and
nothing else.

Then the loss: thirty-two predictions, thirty-two right answers, one cost each,
averaged into one number for the whole batch.

Both steps have a detail that only makes sense with the code in front of you —
which direction the totals are added up in, and why those totals have to keep
their shape when they are divided back out. The cells at the foot of the page

</aside>

## The number training has to beat

A loss of 2.5 means nothing by itself. It only means something beside the loss of
a model that knows nothing at all, so that is the number worth having before
training starts.

Here is how to get it. Take the thirteen-by-thirteen grid — the one every
character's row is fetched from — and set every number in it to zero.

A grid of zeros gives every character in every row the same score. Equal scores
come out of softmax as equal percentages, so every character is given the same
one-in-thirteen chance, whatever came before it. The loss of being that unsure is
`-log(1/13)`, which is `ln(13)`, which is **2.5649**. The lab prints exactly that.

Filling the grid with random numbers instead does *worse* — a loss of 2.8912.
That is not a bug. A grid of zeros is uselessly humble, but a random grid is
confident, and confident about the wrong things, and confidence is what costs:

| chance given to the right answer | `1 - p` | `-log p` |
|---|---|---|
| 0.9 — nearly right | 0.10 | 0.11 |
| 0.4 — unsure | 0.60 | 0.92 |
| 0.01 — confidently wrong | 0.99 | 4.61 |

Both columns are zero for a perfect answer, so why the logarithm? Look at the
bottom two rows. Going from unsure to confidently wrong, `1 - p` grows 1.65
times and `-log p` grows 5.03 times. `1 - p` can never rise above 1 however bad
the answer gets, so a disaster barely registers. `-log p` has no ceiling.

<div class="claim mt-8">
Training moves fastest where the loss rises fastest. A confident mistake has to
hurt, or nothing corrects it.
</div>

## It still only sees one character

None of this bought any context. The model here reads one character and predicts
the next, exactly like the counted bigram — same window, same blindness,
[same `sathe`](/tiny-gpt/02-counting-pairs/#why-is-it-nonsense-if-every-pair-is-legal).

What changed is that its table can be corrected. That is the whole of the
progress, and it is enough to build on: next it gets trained, and after that it
finally gets to look further back than one character.
