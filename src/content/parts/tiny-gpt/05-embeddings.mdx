The dataset is finished. There are questions, there are answers, and there
is a function that produces as many as you like. What there still is not is
anything that can **learn**. The counting model was a tally, with nothing in it
to adjust.

So: build one that can. It fails immediately, on a problem left standing back
when [characters first became numbers](/tiny-gpt/03-into-numbers/#the-numbers-are-labels-not-quantities).

## The numbers were never quantities

In the vocabulary, `t` is 9 and `a` is 1. Feed those into
arithmetic and you have told the model three things, all of them false: that
`t` is nine times `a`, that `t` and `s` are nearly the same because 9 and 8 are
adjacent, and that `d` sits neatly between `c` and `e`.

None of that is about language. It is about alphabetical order, which is how
the numbers were assigned. A model doing sums on them will find that structure,
because it is really there in the numbers, and it means nothing.

<div class="claim mt-7">
The integers identify characters. They do not describe them. Arithmetic cannot
tell the difference.
</div>

## Give each character a row instead

Here is the fix, and you have already built the thing it produces.

Instead of replacing `t` with the number 9, replace it with an entire **row** of
numbers — as wide as the vocabulary, holding one score for every character that
might come next. Stack one row per character and you have a table, vocabulary
tall and vocabulary wide.

That is the shape of [the table you built by counting](/tiny-gpt/02-counting-pairs/#what-does-count-the-pairs-mean). Exactly the
shape. The only difference is where the numbers in it come from.

Counting produced a table that is accurate about one sentence and can never be
anything else. This table starts as noise — genuinely meaningless, and you can
roll it again to prove it — but every number in it can be nudged. The next part does
the nudging. This one gets everything into position for it.

<aside class="aside measure mt-5">
The table is called an **embedding table**, and looking a character up in it is
an *embedding*. The word suggests something more elaborate than a table lookup.
It is a table lookup. Later parts make the rows wider than the vocabulary and
let them carry more than next-character scores, which is where the name starts
to earn itself.
</aside>

## Looking up a whole batch at once

Batching hands over a rectangle of integers — rows of characters, several rows at
a time. Indexing the table with that whole rectangle replaces every integer with
its row in a single expression, turning `(4, 8)` integers into `(4, 8, 13)`
scores.

No loop. This is precisely what a library's embedding layer does inside, and
writing it once is the reason the library version will never be mysterious.

## Scores, not probabilities

What comes out of that lookup are **logits**: raw scores, one per possible next
character. Any real number, negative included, adding up to nothing in
particular.

Leaving them raw is deliberate. It is more numerically stable than having the
model produce probabilities directly, and it is exactly what the loss function
wants to be given. Converting them is a separate step, and it is one you have
already seen.

## Softmax and cross-entropy, on a rectangle

Both of these are explained from scratch in the other tutorial, and they work
here exactly as they do there:

- **[Softmax](/neural-networks/05-how-wrong/)** — exponentiate every score, then
  divide by the total. Everything becomes positive, order is preserved, and the
  result sums to 1.
- **[Cross-entropy](/neural-networks/05-how-wrong/)** — look only at the
  probability the model gave the character that actually came next, and take
  `-log` of it. The rest of the distribution is ignored.

What is new is the shape. There, one drawing produced one row of scores. Here
there are four rows of eight positions, and each position needs its own
independent softmax across the vocabulary — never mixing across positions, never
across rows. That is what `axis=-1` is for, and why `keepdims=True` matters: the
sum has to stay `(4, 8, 1)` so it lines up against `(4, 8, 13)`. Drop it and the
division is a shape error.

For the loss, the two axes get flattened together first — 4 rows of 8 becomes 32
independent predictions — because "32 predictions" is easier to hold in your
head than a pair of axes.

## What an untrained model should score

Worth knowing before training starts, because it tells you whether anything is
working.

A table of zeros gives every character the same score, so every character gets
the same probability: one in thirteen. The loss of that is `-log(1/13)`, which
is `ln(13)`, which is **2.5649**. The lab prints exactly that.

Random numbers do *worse* — 2.8912 for the table in the lab. That is not a bug.
A random table is confident about things at random, and confident mistakes are
expensive:

| probability given to the right answer | `1 - p` | `-log p` |
|---|---|---|
| 0.9 — nearly right | 0.10 | 0.11 |
| 0.4 — unsure | 0.60 | 0.92 |
| 0.01 — confidently wrong | 0.99 | 4.61 |

Both are zero for a perfect answer, so the obvious question is why bother with a
logarithm. The last two rows answer it. Going from *unsure* to *confidently
wrong*, `1 - p` grows 1.65 times and `-log p` grows 5.03 times. `1 - p` can
never exceed 1 no matter how bad things get, so it barely reacts to a disaster.
`-log` has no ceiling.

<div class="claim mt-8">
Training moves fastest where the loss is steepest. A confident mistake has to
hurt, or nothing corrects it.
</div>

## It still only sees one character

Nothing here bought any context. The model in this part looks at one character
and predicts the next, exactly like the counted bigram — same window, same
blindness, [same `sathe`](/tiny-gpt/02-counting-pairs/#why-is-it-nonsense-if-every-pair-is-legal).

What changed is that its table can be corrected. That is the whole of the
progress, and it is enough to build on: next it gets trained, and after that it
finally gets to look further back than one character.
